As foreigners operating a Partnership LLC based in /out of the US, you will need to fill out the W-8BEN form rather than the W-9 form. The W-8BEN is used to certify that you are a foreign person and to claim a reduced rate of withholding tax, if applicable./ if not leave it.

The W-8BEN form requires you to provide your personal information, including your name, country of residence, and taxpayer identification number. You will also need to provide information about the partnership, such as the name, address, and employer identification number (EIN)- if applicable/ if not leave it.

Unlike the W-9 form, the W-8BEN does not require a US person signature at the end. Instead, you will need to sign and date the form, and provide your capacity in which you are signing (e.g., partner of the LLC).

Your LLC is a disregarded entity unless you filed a check the box form to treat it as a corporation. And the answer is, why, of course I filed to treat it as a corporation. The reason you made this election is because of the Anson vs. HMRC case which slammed home to dual qualified US/UK corporate accountants the fact that the HMRC does not honour the US/UK income tax treaty in respect of LLCs and S-corps. In that case, Anson won… but he’s the only person to ever have won. The HMRC put so many restrictions on their ruling that unless you manage to mimic ever single aspect of that case, you’re in for a double tax whammy if the HMRC ever decides to investigate your taxes.

So, given that you are now highly incentivized to file a back-dated check the box election to treat your LLC as a corporation (you can backdate up to three years), you’ll be filing a W9 (since it’s a US corp). Another annoying surprise is that you’ll also be filing UK corporate tax returns and paying UK corporate tax less the US tax already paid. You’ll be filing these UK corporate tax returns since the company is managed and controlled by a UK resident person. You only have to do so in years the corporation made a profit… but if you don’t file it in years of losses, you don’t get to carry those losses forward into the profitable years.

It depends on how the LLC elects to be treated for US tax purposes. If the LLC elects to be treated as a corporation for tax purposes, then the entity uses Form W-9. If the LLC elects default treatment as a disregarded entity for tax purposes, then the appropriate Form W-8 should be provided.

Regulated industries like banking or healthcare have a narrower design space than most AI teams realize. The constraints aren't just technical, they're legal. Here's what works in practice:

No data leaves the perimeter. This is non-negotiable for most regulated entities. If your agent compute runs in a third-party cloud, data flows through their infrastructure and that creates compliance exposure. The architecture needs to run entirely inside your own VPC: compute, logs, audit trails, and key management included.

Scoped, ephemeral credentials per workload. A single shared service account for all agents is a compliance and security liability. Each agent workload should get its own Open ID Connect (OIDC)-compliant identity at launch, scoped only to what it needs, revoked at teardown. This gives you both least-privilege access and a clean audit trail per session.

MCP Gateways instead of direct credential access. Rather than giving agents direct access to databases or APIs, proxy tool calls through a Model Context Protocol (MCP) gateway authenticated by workload identity. The gateway holds the real credential, so the agent never sees it. This is especially important when agents call external services, keeping credentials out of the agent's context entirely.

Deterministic egress controls. Outbound traffic should be restricted to an explicit allowlist at the network layer. This contains the blast radius of a compromised agent and satisfies auditors who need to demonstrate that data can't be exfiltrated to arbitrary endpoints.

Full auditability. Compliance teams need to answer "what did this agent do, when, and under what identity?" Per-call logs tied to workload identity make that answerable.

A2A. It depends.

The J-2 visa is granted to spouses or unmarried dependent children under the age of 21. As such, the individual’s status is derived from the primary visitor’s J-1 status, and the same rules that apply to the J-1 visa holder apply to the J-2 holder.

A J-1 or J-2 visa holder is considered to be an exempt individual, for the purpose of determining days of US residence, unless the visa holder has been exempt for any part of 2 of the 6 calendar years prior to the current year (which can be extended up to four years under certain circumstances). If the visa holder is not an exempt individual, the visa holder uses the substantial presence test to determine US residency status, excluding days in which the visa holder was an exempt individual. As a practical matter, this means that someone who is not currently exempt and who has been in the US for 183 days in the tax year is a resident, regardless of prior year exempt status, but you need to check this for your specific circumstance.

If you hold a J-2 visa as a nonresident alien spouse of a J-1 visa holder who is a resident alien under the terms described above, and you choose to file a joint return, you must elect to be treated as a resident alien for tax purposes.

Once you have validated your own residency status under the terms described above, the form that you file is determined. If you remain a nonresident alien for tax purposes, you provide Form W-8BEN. If you are a resident alien for tax purposes, you provide Form W-9.

Note that if you are working in the US under a J-2 visa, you are generally not exempt from US Social Security and Medicare taxes.

First of all, understand the purpose of Form W-9.

A payer requests the tax ID number of a payee who is a US person or entity by asking the payee to provide Form W-9. The payer requests it because the payer needs the tax ID number to satisfy an IRS filing obligation; if the payer does not receive one, the payer may be obligated to withhold a certain percentage of a payment to the payee for tax purposes. So in this context, all that matters is who the beneficial owner of the payment is; it does not matter whether that person has employees or how that person receives the income, it only matter that the person receives the income.

For a payment to a single-owner LLC, and assuming that the LLC has not elected to be treated as a corporation, the beneficial owner of the payment is the individual owner of that LLC, and not the LLC itself. If the single owner is a foreign person, then that person does not provide Form W-9 (which is only for US “persons”), but rather one of the variants of Form W-8 (which could be either Form W-8BEN or Form W-8ECI).

For a payment to a multiple-owner LLC, or to an LLC that has elected corporate treatment, the beneficial owner of the payment is the LLC. If it's US-created, the W-9 would include the business’s EIN and would typically be signed by the managing partner of the LLC.
